EXOPLANET TRANSIT PHOTOMETRY: SCIENCE FAIR RESEARCH GUIDE

Complete project documentation, hypothesis, formulas, and judge presentation talking points

1. Project Abstract & Core Hypothesis

Problem Statement: Distant exoplanets are too small and faint to photograph directly with conventional telescopes. How can astronomers measure the size, orbital distance, and habitability of planets orbiting distant stars hundreds of light-years away?

Hypothesis: By measuring high-precision time-series changes in stellar brightness (Transit Photometry) and applying Kepler’s Third Law of Planetary Motion, we can accurately deduce an exoplanet's physical radius ($R_p$), semi-major axis ($a$), and surface equilibrium temperature ($T_{eq}$) to determine whether it resides within the circumstellar Habitable Zone.

2. Mathematical Physics & Formulas Applied

Transit Depth Equation

δ = \frac{\Delta F}{F} = \left(\frac{R_p}{R_*}\right)^2

The fractional dip in starlight is directly equal to the ratio of the cross-sectional area of the planet to the host star.

Planet Radius Derivation

R_p = R_* \cdot \sqrt{\delta}

Calculates the physical radius of the exoplanet in Earth Radii ($R_\oplus$) or Jupiter Radii ($R_{Jup}$).

Kepler's Third Law

a = \left(\frac{G \cdot M_* \cdot P^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}

Relates orbital period ($P$) and star mass ($M_*$) directly to the planet-star distance in Astronomical Units (AU).

Equilibrium Temperature ($T_{eq}$)

T_{eq} = T_* (1 - A)^{1/4} \sqrt{\frac{R_*}{2a}}

Assumes planetary albedo ($A \approx 0.3$) and uniform heat redistribution to compute surface temperature in Kelvin.

5. Expected Judge Questions & Answers

Q: Why does the bottom of the light curve stay flat instead of being a V-shape?

A: A flat-bottomed U-shape occurs when the planet is completely inside the disc of the star during mid-transit. A V-shaped light curve usually indicates a grazing transit where the planet only clips the edge of the stellar disc, or an eclipsing binary star system.

Q: What is the biggest source of error in transit photometry?

A: Stellar activity (such as starspots and flares) and instrumental photon noise. Starspots can mimic or distort transits, which is why multi-orbit averaging and space telescopes like Kepler/TESS are used to achieve parts-per-million photometric accuracy.
